## 1. What is a cash flow statement?

A cash flow statement records one thing: the fact of cash actually moving in and out.

Say a company lands a big order worth $1,000, but payment comes 60 days later. During that time it still has to pay $800 in wages and supplier costs.

- Income statement: the $1,000 is already booked as revenue, the $800 is cost, so the paper gross profit is $200. It looks profitable.
- Cash flow statement: the $1,000 has not arrived, the $800 has gone out; free cash flow is minus $800, negative.

Same company, same period. The income statement says profit, the cash flow statement says the cash is gone.

## 2. Why look at the cash flow statement first?

Of the three financial statements, the income statement uses accrual accounting and the balance sheet contains estimates like depreciation. Both carry assumptions. The cash flow statement is different: it only records the fact of cash actually moving, with no estimation.

## 3. How do you read free cash flow (FCF)?

FCF is the cash earned from operations, minus the capital spending on equipment, and whatever is left.

- Positive FCF: the core business earns enough that, after paying for expansion, there is still a surplus. Healthy.
- Negative FCF: you have to look at why. Building a plant or adding capacity is normal in a growth phase; the core business unable to sustain its own cash is the warning sign.

## 4. Profitable and still able to go bankrupt

The moment the order is signed, the amount is booked as revenue and the books look good. But within those 60 days the customer's payment has not come in, while wages and supplier bills keep falling due. If the company does not have enough cash on hand or enough financing capacity to get through those 60 days, it fails. Profitable on paper, but the cash has already run out.

## 5. How this plays out badly in the real world

During the pandemic, some large retailers pushed suppliers to extend payment terms from monthly settlement to 60 or 90 days. Small and mid-sized suppliers had to accept it to keep the relationship. Worse, a large buyer can deliberately delay payment to make a small supplier's cash flow even harder. The supplier has revenue on paper but fails because it cannot collect, and the large buyer then acquires it cheaply. In effect, the buyer uses its own payment-term policy to slowly move the other company's assets into its own hands.
