## 1. You cannot decide when renewables generate

How much a renewable source generates depends on the natural conditions at that moment. Solar depends on sunlight, which varies a lot by latitude; and even at a fixed latitude, clouds and seasons still swing the output up and down. Wind is the same. None of this can be dispatched the way a traditional plant (a coal unit, say) is started up the moment demand rises.

## 2. Regulation keeps pushing the share up, so storage is no longer optional

A traditional grid balances supply and demand with coal and gas units: run more when demand is high, fewer when it is low, using the units' flexibility to hedge the swings in consumption.

But the renewables share keeps rising, and that is not the market's natural choice; it is locked in by regulation: the EU's binding renewable-energy targets, Taiwan's large-user clause that forces big consumers to build or buy green power. The higher the share, the fewer traditional units left to fill in, while the absolute size of the swings gets larger because the renewables share is high. The flexibility of traditional units is no longer enough to hedge that gap.

This problem has no "don't do it" exit. Regulation has locked in the renewables path, physics does not disappear on its own, and the only answer is storage: store the surplus when generation is high, and release it when generation falls short but demand is still there.

## 3. The cost of too little storage: curtailment and a steeper peak

- **Curtailment**: during the day, solar output jumps well above current demand, and with nowhere to store it, the excess has to be dumped. You paid to build generating capacity and then let part of the output go to waste.
- **The duck curve**: in the morning, once solar comes on, the net load on traditional units drops sharply; in the evening, the sun sets and demand rises at the same time (the after-work peak), so traditional units have to ramp up a large amount of output in a very short window. That ramp rate is a big strain on the grid.

These two problems are essentially the same thing: generation time does not line up with consumption time, and without storage as a buffer, the higher the share, the worse it gets.

## 4. AI's power demand is exploding, and storage capacity decides national strength

What AI really needs is two things: chips and power. Chip manufacturing is itself a power-hungry industry. Cleanrooms held at constant temperature and humidity, ultra-pure water systems, lithography equipment running continuously; a fab runs on power essentially year-round. Once the chips are made, data-center compute and cooling are another layer of demand.

AI data centers are creating a large baseload demand that will not come back down, stacked on top of a supply-demand system that has become more fragile as the renewables share rises. Whichever country can build out storage directly decides whether it can supply power stably to AI compute. Storage is going from "an add-on measure for renewables" to "critical national infrastructure for the AI era".
