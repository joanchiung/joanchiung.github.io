## 1. A token's value ultimately comes down to demand

Why can some tokens climb for a decade while others go to zero? A token is an asset issued, recorded, and traded on a blockchain using a smart contract: the blockchain is a public ledger, the smart contract is a set of automated rules written into it. And what decides whether a token climbs for ten years or goes to zero is whether there is "demand". With demand, a price forms; if nobody needs it, the value and the price disappear together.

## 2. Three ways to hold value up

- **Asset-backed**: the issuer holds dollars or treasury bonds as reserves and issues USDT, promising 1:1 redemption. The redemption rule is hardcoded into the contract. Most regular users just buy and sell at market price on an exchange; it is usually institutions that redeem 1:1 directly with the issuer.
- **Utility-backed**: a liquidity pool is where people swap currencies. An LP token records your share of the pool, and from that share the protocol can compute exactly how much profit you are entitled to.
- **Belief-backed**: an NFT is a certificate that proves the uniqueness of an artwork. It is similar to why a Van Gogh painting is valuable. The painting itself is hard to move and trade, so what people actually transfer is the certificate, proving who owns the painting now. Authentication still comes down to people (a professional organization); blockchain's role is to make the certificate tamper-proof and easy to transfer once authentication is done.

USDT, the LP token, and the NFT map to asset, utility, and belief respectively.

## 3. These three are actually a spectrum

The closer to the asset end, the more there is something real holding it up, and the better it withstands pressure. The closer to the belief end, the faster it collapses the moment people stop believing.

- Asset-backed is usually tied to reality (USDT is pegged to the dollar, and the dollar is legal tender enforced by national law), but trust in any individual issuer is not absolute; USDT's reserves have been questioned before over whether they are fully backed.
- Utility-backed is like a trading business. If that business stops being profitable, demand drops, and value and price fall with it, the way some real-world industries slowly become sunset industries.
- The most fragile is belief-backed. Dutch tulip mania is the real-world case: the whole thing ran on people believing tulips were valuable, and once people stopped believing, the bubble burst. NFTs and meme coins run on the same collective attention, with an even thinner connection to reality.

## 4. Vision can come first, but staying power depends on whether it lands

A lot of things start out fine on vision alone: paint a picture of a future first, and that pulls in belief and capital. But the real support point is whether that vision eventually lands as an actual business model.

The internet was the same when it first appeared: everyone thought it could do everything, and only after going through a bubble did it get tightly integrated into business and become indispensable. If something stays nothing more than "flip it and profit", with no connection to any real need, it turns into a bubble easily.
