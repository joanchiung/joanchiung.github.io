## 1. What is a balance sheet?

An income statement is "a report card for a period of time". A balance sheet is "a snapshot on a single day".

Someone born rich goes to work like everyone else, so their income statement looks about the same as their peers'. But look at their balance sheet and the gap is huge: assets several times larger, and asset types other people do not have (an inherited house, far more financial assets). So if you want to know whether a company is structurally sound, or whether it might fail to collect a final payment, you look at the balance sheet.

## 2. The accounting equation: how to read it

Assets = liabilities + equity. The left side is what the company has; the right side is where it came from. Liabilities are borrowed; equity belongs to shareholders.

- Assets: look at "what the company has, and how fast it can turn into cash". $1M in cash and $1M in a factory are the same amount with completely different liquidity.
- Liabilities: look at "who is owed, and when it has to be repaid". Less debt is not automatically better. Borrowing is worth it when the return beats the interest, as long as you can repay.
- Equity: the result of assets minus liabilities. It is an accumulation over the years (retained earnings).

## 3. Coinbase vs NVIDIA

Last week I looked at market cap and the two were more than a hundred times apart. This week I looked at total assets and they are only nine times apart. That gap is itself information: the balance sheet records what a company "has", the market cap reflects what the market thinks it is "worth", and the space between them is the market's imagination about future profit.

NVIDIA's business model is very healthy: a debt ratio of only 25%, three-quarters of its assets funded by shareholders' own money, and 95% of equity built up from years of accumulated net income. The two companies carry almost the same amount of long-term debt, but NVIDIA is nine times bigger. NVIDIA is not unable to borrow; it earns so much it simply does not need to.

Coinbase's debt ratio of 53% looks high, but part of it is a feature of the business model: customer funds held in custody show up on both the asset and the liability side, the way a bank holds money for its customers.

## 4. Replacing trust with verification

In the real world, financial statements can be trusted because government authority and accounting audits stand behind them; faking them gets you jailed. The weakness of that system is that it is slow: one statement per quarter. Traditional companies can live with that, but a crypto asset can move more than 10% in a day, and one report a quarter is far too late.

What blockchain wants to do is swap "trust in public authority" for "verifiable at any moment": you can check the books on-chain whenever you like and confirm the assets are there.

But on-chain verification cannot replace regulation yet, because on-chain you can only see "assets", not "liabilities". In terms of the accounting equation, with only the left side and no right side, you cannot compute equity and you cannot see the whole company. That is exactly why nobody could see the problem before FTX collapsed.

So my conclusion is: transparency can only reinforce regulation for now, not replace it. But the direction is right: replace trust in people with rules wherever you can. There is a phrase in crypto, "Don't trust, verify", and that is the whole point of this.
