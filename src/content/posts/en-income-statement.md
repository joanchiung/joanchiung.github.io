## What is an income statement?

An income statement is a company's report card. It tells you how the company makes money, how much it made, and whether it is healthy. You read it from top to bottom: revenue at the top, various costs below. By layering and categorizing the costs, you get gross profit, then operating profit, then pre-tax profit, all the way down to net income.

## How do you read it?

Top to bottom: revenue minus raw materials and manufacturing cost is gross profit; gross profit minus marketing and staff cost is operating profit; add and subtract non-operating items (currency swings, interest) to get pre-tax profit; subtract tax and you finally have real net income.

Growth shows in revenue, health shows in operating profit, traps show in the gap around net income, and the share price rides on the outlook. Looking at all four directions together tells you far more about a company's real state than staring at the last number alone.

## Why can't you just look at net income?

The number I care about most is net margin, but I always look at operating margin alongside it, because net income is easily swayed by non-operating items, while operating profit reflects the true state of the core business.

More importantly, products have a life cycle. When a company is early and pushing into the market, it needs heavy marketing spend to get consumers to buy in. The income statement looks unprofitable, but that does not mean the company is weak. Netflix was exactly like this. Early on it would rather subsidize consumers and spend big on marketing to get people used to the idea of streaming video. Now when people think of streaming, Netflix is the first name that comes to mind.

## NVIDIA vs Coinbase

This time I actually went and read both companies' filings, and it turned out to be a very blunt comparison. NVIDIA makes money on both its core business and its investments, with net income rising sharply. It is a textbook growth-stage company. Coinbase is the opposite: a small loss on the core business and a large loss on investments. If you only look at net income, it is easy to misjudge both companies. You have to separate the core business from the non-operating side.

What shocked me most was the market cap. Coinbase is worth about $46 billion, NVIDIA more than $5 trillion, a gap of over a hundred times. "The crypto exchange that looks so profitable" versus "the most profitable tech company", and the gap in market value is that wide.

## My take

Before I read these filings, crypto had always struck me as "high risk, a shot at making a lot". After reading Coinbase's filing, it felt completely different. Crypto companies are still in a very early trial-and-error phase and have not found their most stable way to make money. Compare that with NVIDIA, already deep in a product phase that keeps growing insanely, and the two are simply not at the same stage.

That reminds me of how media decentralized. TV stations were once the only channel for promotion; a small business that wanted to market itself had to spend big to get on TV. After Facebook showed up, everyone could promote themselves, and the concentrated money advantage of TV stations was scattered fast. Finance is walking a similar path, just more slowly, because banks have a moat built out of law and risk control.

The bank's role today is like a security door, keeping problem people outside, but sometimes also shutting out promising opportunities. Crypto is like tearing the door off entirely, letting everyone in, but just as unable to keep bad actors out. Countries legislating step by step right now are trying to fit a better door.

My current job sits right at the front edge of this shift: helping the traditional financial system take in crypto, and giving it a safer way to be used. The process is still very early, but that is exactly why I think this position is worth staying in.
