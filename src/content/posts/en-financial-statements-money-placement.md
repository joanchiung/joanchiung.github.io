## 1. Financial statements are my tool for deciding "where the money goes"

I do not really use financial statements to pick a single stock. To me they are more like a manual for "how the world is going to develop next", and what I actually use them for is deciding where to put my money. Investing is always buying the future, and people put money where the need is strongest and the hope is greatest.

So when I read a statement I am not fixated on the numbers. I am looking at which people and which companies are doing what business, and how they make money. I split the money into two parts: one for "offense" chasing growth, one for "defense" seeking stability.

## 2. Look at the world's real needs first, then follow the supply chain

For the offense part, I look at the world's real needs first. Big trends like aging populations and political direction throw off certain needs that "have to be solved". I first think about which industries those needs map to, then use financial statements to see: along this supply chain, who is providing the solution, and where is the capital deployed.

An example: the whole world is facing an aging population, with a labor shortage on one side and care demand only rising on the other, so robotics is close to an irreversible trend. And for robots to actually be usable, they need safe control systems, recognition and sensing, logic that can make judgments, even emotional interaction behind them. Follow those needs down and you arrive at key optoelectronic components, industrial applications, and transmission technology.

## 3. Use stable industries as a floor, but use the statements to pick the strongest one

For the defense part, I put it in more stable industries, like finance. In Taiwan, state-owned banks are less likely to fail and less volatile, because the government is a major shareholder and provides an implicit backstop. But "will not fail" and "structurally strong" are two different things.

You first have to understand how a bank makes money: it really earns by lending out deposits and taking the spread, so its biggest risk is "loans it cannot collect". To judge a bank's health, first look at the non-performing loan ratio (the bad-debt ratio), then pair it with the capital adequacy ratio (the cushion for absorbing losses), and ROE.

So my defense has two layers: first pick a stable industry, then use these few numbers to pick the one with the strongest fundamentals.
