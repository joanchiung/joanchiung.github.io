## 1. "Good" only shows up when you compare

A number on a financial statement by itself only tells you a "fact", not a "performance".

Two shops both sell for $100: one sells ice cream (cost $20, keeps $80), the other sells lunchboxes (cost $60, keeps $40). Looking only at "both sell for $100" you would think they are about the same. Convert it to "how much is left for every $100 sold" and the gross margin is twice as far apart. A single number means little; you have to turn it into a ratio to compare. And not just against others, but against its own past and against its peers.

## 2. Four steps

The first three steps ask "is this a good business"; the last asks "is it worth buying now".

- **Is it good** (earning efficiency, pricing power): ROE, net margin
- **Is it stable** (could it fail): debt-to-equity (threshold under 1, conservative under 0.5, compare with peers)
- **Is it real** (is the paper profit actually cash): free cash flow
- **Is it expensive**: P/E (a P/E of 8 is roughly an 8-year payback, a P/E of 40 roughly 40 years; a general threshold under 15, read alongside PEG)

## 3. Standing on the shoulders of giants

These thresholds are not something I invented: ROE above 15%, P/E below 15, are what Buffett and Graham used. "Cheap stocks perform better over the long run" has also been tested by large-scale academic research (Fama-French). When I do not know how to choose, starting from these widely accepted principles saves a huge amount of time versus researching from zero. It is not a crystal ball, but it filters out the ones that are obviously no good.

If I were doing short-term momentum investing, the ratios I would look at would be completely different. So the point is: figure out your own investing habits first, then pick the matching standards.

## 4. Connecting to blockchain: the same logic, reinvented

Crypto prices are mostly driven up by emotion and narrative, and many tokens are not earning anything, so over the past few years people have started moving traditional ratios on-chain:

- **Real yield**: protocols used to prop up high yields by minting new tokens (like printing shares to pay a dividend, which is fake). Real yield insists the yield comes from fees the protocol actually collects. That is the crypto version of FCF.
- **Price-to-sales / price-to-earnings**: platforms like Token Terminal compute exactly this. It is essentially the P/E ratio moved on-chain.

The same logic of "first ask whether there is real cash, then ask whether it is expensive now" is being reinvented even in the newest field.
